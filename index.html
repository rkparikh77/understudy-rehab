A rehab robot that learns therapy from the therapist's own hands.

After a stroke, the arm needs hundreds of guided repetitions a day. A therapy session delivers about 30. Understudy records how a therapist assists a patient, learns that technique, and repeats it at home, helping only as much as the patient needs.

The problem

Recovery needs dose. The system can't deliver it.

Relearning movement after a stroke depends on high-volume, task-specific practice. Hands-on therapy is the bottleneck: a therapist can only guide one arm at a time, and outpatient visits end long before recovery does.

How it works

Teach once in clinic. Practice hundreds of times at home.

The robot attaches at the forearm and is light and backdrivable, so a therapist can move it as easily as the patient's own arm. Their hands become the training data.

1 · clinic

Teach

The therapist guides the patient's arm through an exercise. The robot records position and the force the therapist applied, at every point.

2 · overnight

Learn

An imitation-learning policy (action-chunking or diffusion) trains on a few dozen demonstrations. It learns when this therapist helps and when they let go.

3 · home

Replay

The patient practices daily. The robot assists only when the hand drifts off the taught path, so the patient does as much of the work as they can.

4 · remote

Supervise

Assist force falling over weeks is a direct recovery measure. The therapist reviews it remotely and re-teaches when the plan changes.

Why now

Past rehab robots failed on cost and rigidity. Both have changed.

Earlier systems needed industrial hardware and hand-coded trajectories. The pieces now exist to make one cheap enough for a home and adaptive enough to be worth using.

Hardware fell by an order of magnitude

Open-source arms built from hobby servos now run modern robot-learning stacks. A rehab-grade version with stronger actuators and a force-sensing cuff is a parts list in the low thousands of dollars.

Robots learn from a few dozen demonstrations

Since 2023, imitation learning has gone from research to routine, with open toolkits that train useful policies from tens of human demonstrations. Therapists already teach by demonstration.

Payers already reimburse supervised home therapy

Medicare's remote therapeutic monitoring codes pay clinics to supervise patients' home exercise, and the 2026 fee schedule added shorter-duration codes. The billing rails exist; the device that makes the time worth billing doesn't.

Therapist time is the scarce resource

Rehab staffing shortages mean fewer visits per patient. A tool that lets one therapist supervise many patients' daily practice addresses the constraint directly.

Business model

Lease to clinics. Paid for by work they can already bill.

Our customer is the outpatient rehab clinic or home-health agency, not the patient. Clinics lease Understudy units monthly and send them home with patients after discharge. Each unit lets a therapist keep treating a patient between and after visits, and that supervision is billable.

The long-term asset is the data. Every taught session pairs an expert's hands-on technique with a patient's recovery curve, a dataset that does not exist anywhere today. It lets us train a shared rehab policy so every clinic starts from the best technique, and gives payers an objective measure of recovery to justify coverage.

Safety

Designed to be safe to hold

The robot is low-force and backdrivable, so a patient or therapist can always overpower it. Force is capped in hardware and software, assistance only pushes toward the taught path, and any unexpected resistance stops motion. Home use starts with exercises the therapist has taught and approved in clinic.
